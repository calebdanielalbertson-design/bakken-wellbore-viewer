What is wrong with the September calculation?

  1. Acreage allocation is being used as engineering capacity. The old allocator correctly divides overlapping faces into equal shares and reconciles to the unique land union. But it then converts each DSU’s share into capacity at eight wells per 1,280 acres. Adding an overlapping DSU can reduce another unit’s displayed capacity even though no well was drilled and no rock disappeared. Across 5,517 nominal 1,280-acre units, the median allocated share is 50% and the median default capacity is four wells.
  2. Well assignment and physical occupation are different. Each well is accepted to at most one DSU. A different overlapping unit can therefore show few assigned wells even when many mapped paths cross it. Of 5,970 published September estimates, 2,290 contain at least 90% of a saved mapped path accepted elsewhere; 1,028 show positive remaining with zero assigned wells despite such paths. These are plan-view diagnostics, not proof that every path is in the same producing bench.
  3. The identity join is unreliable independently of the capacity model. The current strict join exposes 759 eligible estimates, 413 review records and 9,401 unmatched records. Of the unmatched records, 9,281 have exactly identical raw coordinate arrays and identity attributes to an old record. Re-running the frozen fingerprint on an old stored polygon can produce a different value from the saved fingerprint. The precise library/projection/canonicalization cause is not isolated. “Unmatched” does not mean the boundary changed.
  4. Count, length equivalents and geological targets are mixed. The imported remaining value subtracts assigned physical wells; a separate old calculation subtracts lateral-length equivalents. They differ in 2,265 published records. The equivalent helper also replaces 408 recorded lengths below 3,000 ft with 10,000 ft. Neither calculation models actual cross-lateral spacing or verifies producing benches. Depth groups and the approximate DSU display interval are not bench assignments.

What the viewer can establish now

Select a DSU to highlight associated wells in cyan and view combined monthly oil. Choose any positive lateral overlap, at least 660 ft, or at least half of a well’s mapped lateral. Operator filters apply to both highlights and totals. Membership uses the original source footprint, exact segment clipping and within-well union of repeated linework; it excludes unsupported MD gaps over 1,000 ft. Insets, exploded display depths and wellhead position do not determine membership.

Production is whole-well reported oil across pools, counted once per selected well. It is not oil allocated to the section of lateral within the DSU. Monthly missingness stays missing; amber chart points show incomplete well reporting. A well may appear in several overlapping DSUs, so their group totals must not be added. Supplied production extends through December 2024, with a historical coverage gap between the 1950s segment and 2000.

A better structure: township reporting, shared location accounting

1 · TownshipA stable reporting and navigation parent.
2 · Actual PLSS landDisjoint sections/cells with measured area and explicit gaps.
3 · Lateral corridorsObserved paths, supported benches and spacing scenarios.
4 · One candidate IDCount once across all overlapping DSU alternatives.
  1. Freeze a land accounting grid. Use actual PLSS township/range/section polygons. Dissolve repeated fragments by full source key and audit cross-key overlaps. Fixed adjacent section pairs provide a nominal 1,280-acre reporting option; partial sections retain their measured area. North–south and east–west layouts are alternatives, not additive inventories. Townships summarize the same conserved land/length ledger; simply summing the old DSU remaining values into townships will not fix them.
  2. Record observed lateral occupation once per well. Union exact shared branches within the same physical well, retain distinct wells even if XY paths coincide, and clip supported lateral length to the disjoint cells. Preserve branch status, completion evidence, uncertain surveys and unknown benches. Near-coincident revisions need a declared tolerance sensitivity rather than silent merging.
  3. Separate hypothetical headroom from actual locations. A density/area minus supported two-mile-equivalent length worksheet is a transparent first screening scenario. Show low/base/high density and 2/3/4-mile reference-length sensitivities. It is not yet a list of drillable wells. A stronger location model must test corridor orientation, cross-track spacing, continuity, edges, existing paths and bench-specific evidence.
  4. Use unique candidate trajectories. A candidate referenced by 1,280-, 2,560- and 3,840-acre overlays is still one candidate. Alternative layouts are mutually exclusive. Keep undrilled permitted locations, drilled/uncompleted wells, and hypothetical infill as separate categories. Eligibility, authorization and eventual development likelihood require their own evidence.
  5. Validate before publishing a replacement total. Start with the two overlap examples and a clean nonoverlapping unit. Test area/length conservation and require duplicate DSU/branch invariance. Then use frozen temporal snapshots and held-out townships/pads. The available September-to-October identifier comparison finds 327 newly observed branches and 151 newly observed permits; these are publication changes, not verified new drilling. They cannot validate a twelve-month development probability.

Why a statewide total would be premature

The current Bakken DSU polygon union measures about 8.124 million acres; naively summing the polygons gives about 17.698 million acres (2.18×). This illustrates overlap, not an error in the existing union-conserving area allocator. The 759 currently available imported estimates cover only 9.86% of that union. Much of the missing coverage reflects the identity issue, but restoring old joins would not validate the remaining-well method. Missing estimates must not be filled with zero or scaled into a statewide forecast.

Verified producing intervals and common elevation references are still needed to distinguish Middle Bakken and Three Forks targets. NDGS’s local development study treats reservoir horizons separately and shows why stacked development cannot be reduced to one basin-wide well density. NDGS Report of Investigation 135.
